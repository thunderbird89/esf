## Why it's avoided for machine control
Federation platforms rarely use neural interfacing, as the technology is deemed to be too noisy under field conditions for reliable control. Unlike the "synchronization" popularized in older mecha fiction, a _Landmate_ or _Talon_ pilot never wires their nervous system directly into the machine; motion capture rigs and force-feedback systems read the pilot's actual limb movement instead, a slower but far more reliable channel once adrenaline, g-forces, and battlefield stress are in the mix. Federation neuroscientists suspect the problem is partly cultural to the technology's origin — it was calibrated against Xenari physiology, and the Xenari never evolved a fight-or-flight response to begin with, so the hardware simply isn't built to filter the kind of adrenal noise a stressed human nervous system produces.

## Where it's trusted
Neural interfacing *is* trusted at a much smaller scale: low-bandwidth implants that read and write information rather than trying to arbitrate direct control of heavy machinery. A garbled word in a comms feed or a flickering HUD glyph is recoverable in a way a garbled steering input to a several-ton exoskeleton is not, so implants are cleared for exactly that kind of use.

## Field use in covert operations
Covert operatives are the heaviest users of the technology, since a hands-free command interface is worth the expense and invasiveness for personnel who can't afford to fumble for a radio or a scope. A typical field implant carries subvocalized communication, night vision and zoom optics fed directly into the visual cortex, and HUD overlays with targeting reticles layered over natural sight. The same implant usually doubles as the control channel for an operative's other cybernetics — reinforced skeletal or muscular augments, subdermal armor weave, or tools like the _ICE Pick_ — letting a single mental command chain replace what would otherwise be a fumbling sequence of manual switches.
