Nearly all Federation firearms use _thermal clips_, machined blocks of a proprietary lithium-beryllium alloy of large heat capacity, to store the waste heat of their operation. Most weapons can cool down naturally over a few minutes if the rate of fire is kept low enough, but a clip can also be ejected and replaced with a fresh one to keep firing immediately.

## How they work
Every shot a weapon fires — kinetic, plasma, or laser — dumps some fraction of its energy back into the gun as waste heat, and a thermal clip's job is simply to absorb that heat and hold it, rather than let it build up in the barrel or receiver. As long as the rate of fire stays low enough for the clip to shed heat back into the surrounding air faster than it accumulates, a weapon never needs to stop firing at all. Push the rate of fire too high for too long, though, and the clip saturates: it's holding as much heat as the alloy can take, and the weapon has to stop.

At that point, a shooter has two options. Ease off and let the clip passively vent — typically a matter of minutes, faster in a cold environment, slower in a hot one — or eject the spent clip outright and slot in a fresh one from a side or under-barrel magazine, restoring full firing capability in about two seconds. The same mechanism scales up: _Guardian_ point-defense turrets carry ten-clip magazines for exactly this reason, trading the six-to-eight second wait for a coolant vent for a two-second clip swap when they need to keep engaging.

## The lithium-beryllium alloy
The choice of lithium and beryllium isn't arbitrary. A solid's capacity to store heat comes down to how much energy its atoms can absorb as vibration, and for most simple solids, the *molar* heat capacity converges on roughly the same value regardless of what the solid is made of — the Dulong–Petit law puts it at about 3R, or 25 joules per mole per kelvin. Because that figure is per mole rather than per gram, it means the heat capacity of a solid *by mass* is inversely proportional to its atomic weight: lighter atoms bank more thermal energy for the same weight of metal. Lithium, at an atomic mass of 6.9, has one of the highest specific heat capacities of any solid element — about 3.58 J/(g·K), roughly eight times that of iron and well over twenty times that of tungsten. Beryllium, at 9.0, isn't far behind at about 1.82 J/(g·K), and brings something lithium lacks: a high melting point, real structural stiffness, and — critically — excellent thermal conductivity, which matters as much as raw capacity. A block that can't move heat away from the point of contact fast enough will scorch and degrade locally long before the rest of its mass has done any useful work absorbing energy.

Pure lithium metal solves neither of those problems on its own: it's soft, low-melting, and dangerously reactive with atmospheric moisture, entirely unsuitable for a component that gets ejected onto open ground. Alloying it with beryllium locks the lithium into a stable intermetallic lattice, trading away a little of the raw specific heat for a block that survives repeated thermal cycling without deforming, igniting, or corroding. The clips are machined rather than cast specifically to control grain structure at manufacture: a fine, uniform grain lets heat entering at the chamber face conduct evenly through the whole block via beryllium's phonon pathways, instead of pooling into a hotspot that saturates — and fails — long before the alloy's theoretical capacity is used up.

## On the battlefield
Thermal clips are cheap to manufacture, and it shows in how they're treated in the field: a spent clip ejected mid-firefight is simply left where it falls, and nobody breaks formation to recover it. Federation logistics doesn't budget for chasing down every dropped clip under fire, and the alloy poses no hazard just sitting on open ground.

That changes once the shooting stops. Post-engagement cleanup details routinely sweep the field for spent clips, not out of caution but economy: a reconditioned clip — discharged of residual heat and re-verified against spec — costs a fraction of a freshly machined one, and lithium-beryllium stock is worth recovering even in bulk. It's a small piece of the Federation's broader habit of treating even its cheapest consumables as recoverable material rather than litter.
