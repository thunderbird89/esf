Plasma turbines are a core propulsion and power-generation method employed by the Federation, using micro fusion power plants to generate thrust outstripping conventional jet engines by several orders of magnitude, capable of operating both inside and outside the atmosphere. They power almost all Federation aerial platforms, giving them a thrust and top speed unparalleled on Earth and on par with alien adversaries.

## How it works
A plasma turbine is a fusion reactor built around a magnetically-confined ring of circulating plasma, which is used to excite injected propellant past the point of ignition and sustain fusion continuously rather than in short pulses. That circulating ring is the core of the design: it isn't just a byproduct of the reaction, but the mechanism that keeps the reaction going and the interface the rest of the engine draws from.

The turbine can draw on that ring in two ways. Left undisturbed, the circulating plasma simply induces current as it moves through the containment field, turning the reactor into a self-contained power plant. Redirected and expelled through a nozzle, the same plasma becomes reaction mass, turning the reactor into a rocket. A plasma turbine can do either, or both, without reconfiguration — the distinction is purely a matter of how much of the ring's output is diverted to thrust at any given moment.

## One engine, two jobs
Conventional platforms need a separate generator and a separate engine, each with its own fuel draw and its own failure points. A plasma turbine collapses both into a single system: the same circulating mass that powers a machine's weapons, shields, sensors, and life support is what its pilot throttles up for thrust when the mission calls for it. This is why a single engineering core can sit at the heart of a modular platform's design — the propulsion and the power grid were never really two systems to begin with.

## Regenerative idle, expensive combat
At idle — running only to generate power, with no thrust being drawn — a plasma turbine is regenerative, sustaining its reaction on a trickle of propellant so small it's effectively negligible. A platform can sit with its turbine "lit" for as long as its crew and stores allow, without meaningfully touching its fuel reserves.

That efficiency disappears the moment real thrust is demanded. Diverting the ring to propulsion means feeding it propellant far faster than the reaction alone consumes, and that draw scales with however much thrust is being asked of it. A _Talon_ on a long patrol can loiter for what is, for practical purposes, an unlimited stretch of time, burning almost nothing until it needs to maneuver in combat — at which point its fuel gauge moves fast, and by a lot. A _Landmate_ shows the same curve on a longer timescale: standing watch or walking patrol, its engineering core can run for years without a refuel, but the moment it's fighting — shields up, weapons cycling, jump jets firing — that same core is burning through propellant at a rate that would drain it in hours rather than years.

## Deployment across platforms
The full-scale version of the turbine, sized for sustained high-speed flight, drives nearly every Federation aircraft: the _Talon_'s core plasma engine, the _Aeon_'s aft-mounted pair, the _Scorpion_'s hybrid air-breathing/plasma nacelles, and the _Aurora_'s four high-performance units all trace back to the same underlying design, scaled and tuned for their airframe.

Smaller, purely generator-mode variants — sized down as micro-fusion cores rather than full turbines, since nothing needs to be expelled as thrust — show up wherever a platform needs sustained power without needing to fly: the _Goliath_'s fusion core, and the micro-fusion cores that back up the battery systems in covert-ops Q-Cars.
